# Nordic pension systems: how much is actually funded?

*Sweden, Denmark, Norway and Finland. Figures verified August 2026 against national sources and OECD.*

## The question

> **How much of accrued statutory pension rights is backed by actual financial assets?**

**Funded** here means an existing portfolio earmarked for pension financing. A future tax or contribution stream is not funding. Norway's Government Pension Fund Global sits outside the ratio: it is sovereign wealth, not an asset matched to accrued rights.

| Country | Funded share of the statutory PAYG promise |
|---|---|
| **Finland** | **~25–30%** |
| **Sweden** | **~19%**, with a fully funded premium pension on top |
| **Norway** | **~4%** |
| **Denmark** | **Not comparable**—Folkepension accrues no earnings-related liability; ATP is separately funded |

Two distinctions do most of the work. **Statutory rights** describe the legal pillar; **state-managed assets** describe who invests the money—a statutory pension can be privately managed, as in Sweden and Finland. And **funded** is not **defined-contribution**: Finland's statutory DB promise is partly funded, and Swedish insured DB is funded.

## Comparison

| | Sweden | Denmark | Norway | Finland |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main statutory pension | Income pension + premium pension | Folkepension (residence/tax based); ATP the funded supplement | Folketrygden | Earnings-related pension (TyEL and public schemes) |
| Core design | PAYG + buffer funds + funded DC account | PAYG basic + funded ATP + very large occupational pillar | Predominantly PAYG | Partially funded statutory DB |
| Accrued rights | SEK 11,859bn income + supplementary; SEK 2,967bn premium pension (2025) | No accrued earnings-related liability; ATP liabilities DKK 538bn | NOK 10,650bn (2021) | EUR 960.6bn (2023); ~1,100bn end-2025 est. |
| Assets backing them | SEK 2,234bn buffer funds; SEK 3,014bn premium pension | ATP provisions DKK 694bn | NOK 417bn (GPFN, 2025) | EUR 285bn (2025) |
| **Funded share of PAYG promise** | **~19%** | **Not comparable** | **~4%** | **~25–30%** |
| Individual investment choice in the statutory pillar | Yes | No | No | No |
| Funded occupational pillar | Large | Extremely large | Yes, ~74% funded | Mostly inside the statutory system |

Vintages differ: Sweden end-2025, Finland's assets end-2025 against an end-2023 liability, Norway's entitlements end-2021 against end-2025 assets.

## Sweden: partially fund the public pension promise

At end-2025, accrued liabilities were SEK 14,825bn—**SEK 11,859bn** income and supplementary pension, **SEK 2,967bn** premium pension. Financial assets were SEK 5,248bn: **SEK 2,234bn** in the AP buffer funds and **SEK 3,014bn** in the premium pension.

**Don't use the blended ratio.** Dividing all assets by all liabilities gives 35.4%, which is arithmetically correct and analytically empty. The premium pension is a DC account whose liability is *defined* as the market value of the units held, so its funding ratio is 101.6% and cannot be much else.

| Block | Assets (SEK bn) | Liability (SEK bn) | Ratio | Share of liability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Income + supplementary pension | 2,234 | 11,859 | **18.8%** | 80% |
| Premium pension | 3,014 | 2,967 | **101.6%** | 20% |
| Total | 5,248 | 14,825 | 35.4% | 100% |

More than half the blended 35% comes from a block that is fully funded by construction. The figure that answers the question is **18.8%**. Compared like for like, Sweden pre-funds *less* of its statutory promise than Finland—the opposite of what the blended numbers suggest.

Both Swedish numbers matter, as long as they stay apart. A Swedish worker's statutory pension does contain a funded DC component that a Finnish worker's does not.

**Two things the 18.8% doesn't say.** The buffer funds were never meant to fund the liability; they smooth demographic swings in contribution income. And Sweden's own solvency measure adds the contribution asset—the capitalized value of future contributions, SEK 11,370bn—giving total assets of SEK 13,604bn against an SEK 11,859bn liability, a surplus of SEK 1,746bn and a **balance ratio of 1.1472**. No automatic balancing for 2027. The same system is 18.8% financially backed and 114.7% solvent on its statutory measure.

**Housekeeping.** The buffer funds were consolidated effective 1 January 2026: AP1 and AP6 were wound up into AP2, AP3 and AP4, leaving three funds. The SEK 2,234bn is the end-2025 figure, when five existed.

On management: premium pension fund insurance was SEK 2,858bn, of which AP7 held roughly SEK 1,500bn. A little over half sits in the state-run default, a little under half in privately managed funds. The right is statutory either way.

## Finland: collectively pre-fund a statutory DB promise

Statutory pension assets were **EUR 285bn** at end-2025, covering every provider including Keva and VER. The liability side is harder, because no current official figure exists:

| Source | Date | Value | Discount rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statistics Finland | end-2023 | **EUR 960.6bn** (352% of GDP) | 2% real |
| ETK | end-2023 | **EUR 816.7bn** | 2.5% real to 2031, 3.5% after |

Discount rates dominate. Statistics Finland's own sensitivity: EUR 1,159.7bn at 1% real, 960.6bn at 2%, 811.8bn at 3%. A one-point change moves the number by EUR 150–200bn. Extrapolating to end-2025 lands near EUR 1,100bn, but that is an estimate.

The funded share therefore runs **26–30%** depending on the pairing, and 31–35% on ETK's basis. Roughly a quarter to a third is the honest range; a single percentage is really a discount-rate assumption.

The flow picture differs and is worth quoting alongside it: of EUR 37.3bn paid out in 2025, EUR 30.0bn came from contributions and EUR 7.4bn from assets. On that basis Finland runs about **80% pay-as-you-go**.

**Management splits, rights don't.** EUR 181bn (64%) sits with private-sector institutions, EUR 104bn (36%) with public ones—Keva 74, VER 26. None of it is private pension rights; both groups finance compulsory statutory pensions. Finland is the clearest case that legal pension status and asset manager are separate questions.

The two halves are funded differently. Private-sector pensions split into funded and unfunded components with actuarially determined technical provisions the provider must cover. In the public sector, as ETK puts it, accrued rights are determined "but not the share funded from it"—Keva and VER hold buffer reserves aimed at stabilizing contributions. Buffer logic, not actuarial cover.

## Norway: fund the state, not the pension promise

Folketrygden is predominantly PAYG; its *pensjonsbeholdning* is a notional account with no assets behind it.

SSB estimated accrued entitlements at **NOK 10,650bn** at end-2021, 3.2 times mainland GDP. Note that this covers old-age, survivors' *and* disability benefits, not old-age alone. The 2021 vintage is still the latest—it is a three-yearly exercise, and 2024 is unpublished.

The dedicated reserve is the Government Pension Fund Norway: **NOK 417bn at end-2025**. That gives **3.9%**—call it 4%. About 96% of accrued state pension rights are unmatched by earmarked assets.

**On excluding the GPFG.** It stood at **NOK 21,268bn** at end-2025, but carries no defined pension liabilities and finances the budget generally. Counting it would turn a pension comparison into a comparison of government net wealth. One honest qualification: GPFN and GPFG are two legs of the same fund, Statens pensjonsfond, and no formal pension obligations attach to either. Treating one as "the pension reserve" follows OECD convention, not Norwegian law.

**Occupational pensions are the opposite story.** Obligations were **NOK 3,113bn** in 2025, 75.8% DB and 24.2% DC. Of that, NOK 810bn is the central-government SPK scheme, which SPK states plainly is unfunded. The remaining NOK 2,303bn sits predominantly in funded institutions and DC accounts—roughly **74% funded** overall, as a structural estimate rather than an audited ratio.

## Denmark: fund retirement income outside a state liability

Folkepension is residence-based and tax-financed—a full pension needs residence for nine-tenths of the period from age 15 to pension age. It is not purely residence-based in payout: the *pensionstillæg* tapers by 30.9 øre per krone of other taxable income for a single pensioner, and the *ældrecheck* adds up to DKK 26,900 a year, asset- and income-tested. No earnings-related liability accrues, so there is nothing for a funding ratio to divide into.

**ATP is the funded statutory supplement**, compulsory for employees and, since 2020, funded by state contributions on transfer incomes rising to 3.3% by 2030. The headline provisions figure is not the promise:

| ATP, end-2025 | DKK bn |
|---|---|
| Guaranteed pensions | 530.0 |
| Life annuity with market exposure | 7.6 |
| Risk adjustment | 0.6 |
| **Pension liabilities** | **538.1** |
| Long-term supplementary provisions | 39.4 |
| Bonus potential | 116.5 |
| **Total pension provisions** | **694.1** |

The actuarial liability is **DKK 538bn**; the DKK 694bn usually quoted is members' net assets and includes a DKK 117bn free reserve. Coverage of the guaranteed promise is about **129%**. ATP runs 80% of each contribution into guarantees backed by a bond-and-swap hedge and 20% into a bonus portfolio, which returned 19.5% in 2025. It sits outside Solvency II and reports bonus capacity—20.4% at year-end—rather than a solvency ratio.

**Occupational pensions are the largest in the OECD** relative to GDP: **206.4% at end-2024**. The mix has shifted decisively to market-rate products, which overtook average-rate savings in early 2025; collective products with guarantees are down to about 10% of assets. Note ATP sits *inside* the 206.4%, so adding it separately double-counts roughly 23% of GDP.

**Why Denmark's measured liability looks small.** Standardized data show total pension entitlements of 94% of GDP in 2021 and 76% in 2023, against Finland's 336%, Norway's 305% and Sweden's 302%. Partly definitional: Eurostat classifies Folkepension as *social assistance* rather than social insurance, so it is excluded from the entitlement table entirely. Danish taxpayers still owe it; it simply never appears as an accrued liability.

## Broader funded assets

OECD, assets earmarked for retirement, end-2024, % of GDP:

| Denmark | Iceland | Netherlands | Sweden | Finland | Norway |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 206.4 | 191.3 | 150.9 | 115.8 | 65.2 | 10.1 |

**Norway's 10.1% is not comparable with the rest.** OECD publishes no "Total" series for Norway; the figure covers autonomous *pensjonskasser* only. Norwegian life insurers held NOK 2,420bn at end-2024—roughly 47% of GDP—and are excluded, while Denmark's and Sweden's totals are dominated by exactly that vehicle. Read as one series, the row implies Norway has almost no funded pension system, which contradicts its NOK 3,113bn of occupational obligations.

The dataset also excludes public PAYG reserve funds, reported separately: **Finland 35.3%**, **Sweden 33.3%**, **Norway 7.3%**, no entry for Denmark. Norway's 7.3% is GPFN alone—the GPFG was 367% of GDP at end-2024.

These are pools of capital, not funding ratios, and shouldn't be set against national liability measures without checking scope.

## Four answers to one problem

- **Sweden**—partially fund the state pension. PAYG income pension ~19% buffered, fully funded statutory DC account alongside, large occupational pillar.
- **Finland**—partially fund the statutory DB promise. One architecture, a quarter to a third pre-funded, no individual accounts.
- **Norway**—keep pensions PAYG, accumulate sovereign wealth separately. Folketrygden ~4% funded, occupational ~74%, GPFG deliberately outside.
- **Denmark**—keep the basic pension PAYG, fund the supplements. Tax-financed Folkepension, funded ATP, the OECD's largest occupational system.

## Reading the numbers

1. **Discount rates dominate.** Finland's accrued rights swing EUR 350bn between a 1% and a 3% real rate. Eurostat's convention is 4% nominal / 2% real from the 2018 reference year.
2. **Vintages differ.** Norway's end-2021 liability against end-2025 assets flatters the ratio. Only Sweden's figures are matched within one balance-sheet date.
3. **Accrued-to-date is not projected expenditure.** These value rights already earned.
4. **PAYG is not fiscally weak.** "Unfunded" is an asset-liability description, not a solvency judgment. Sweden's income pension is 18.8% backed and comfortably solvent on its own measure.
5. **Sovereign wealth is not pension funding**—but the line is a convention, as the GPFN/GPFG split shows.
6. **Scope varies by publisher.** OECD counts only autonomous pension funds in Norway; Eurostat excludes Folkepension from Denmark. Neither is an error; neither travels well.

Four dimensions get collapsed into one too often: **DB versus DC** (how the benefit is set), **funded versus PAYG** (whether assets exist), **state versus private management** (who invests), and **statutory versus occupational** (which pillar). The Nordics supply a counterexample to every lazy pairing—a partially funded statutory DB system in Finland, a funded statutory DC account in Sweden, funded insured DB, and statutory Finnish assets run by nominally private institutions without becoming private pension rights.

## Bottom line

> **Finland ~25–30% funded; Sweden ~19% for the PAYG income pension with a fully funded premium pension on top; Norway ~4%; Denmark structurally non-comparable, because Folkepension accrues no earnings-related liability while ATP is separately funded at about 129%.**

For pre-funded retirement income overall, the ranking reverses: Denmark leads by a distance, on ATP plus an occupational system worth 206% of GDP. Norway sits at the other end—a barely funded pension promise alongside exceptional state financial wealth held outside the pension system.
